# Delhivery Last-Mile Delay Intelligence Pipeline

A delay-intelligence pipeline built on 1,200 historical parcels from a 24-hub regional network. It estimates delivery hours at dispatch, flags the parcels likely to miss their promise, and sorts the hubs into three operating segments.

The cleaned history has a delay rate of 0.2317. The pipeline exists to find those parcels before they are late instead of counting them afterwards.

## How to run

From a fresh clone, in the repository root:

```powershell
python -m venv .venv
.\.venv\Scripts\Activate.ps1
```

That activation line is for PowerShell. In cmd.exe use `.venv\Scripts\activate.bat`, and on macOS or Linux use `source .venv/bin/activate`.

Then install the pinned packages and run the scripts in this order:

```powershell
pip install -r requirements.txt
python generate_data.py            # optional, the three data/ files are committed
python part_a_clean_eda.py
python part_b_regression.py
python part_c_classification.py
python part_d_segments.py
python package_model.py
python predict_delay.py
```

The order matters. `part_a_clean_eda.py` writes `data/modelling_table.csv`, which Parts B, C and D all read. `package_model.py` reads the configuration Part C chose. `predict_delay.py` reads the four artefacts `package_model.py` writes into `models/`.

`generate_data.py` is optional because the three files in `data/` are committed. Re-running it rewrites the same three files, since every value comes from fixed seeds.

`models/delay_model.pkl` is regenerated by running `python package_model.py`. The committed pickle loads reliably only on the scikit-learn version pinned in `requirements.txt`, because a model pickled by one version may not unpickle correctly in another. Where installed versions differ, the grader re-runs `package_model.py` rather than loading the committed pickle. That rebuilds the model and all four `models/` files from the repository.

## Data findings

All five findings come from the three Part A output files.

### (i) Express misses its promise most often

Express has a delay rate of 0.4897, with 119 of 243 shipments late. Standard is 0.2056 and Economy 0.0857 (`outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`).

Almost half of Express bookings break their promise, so Express is where a proactive call or escalation returns the most per call. Mean Express delivery time is 36.8056 hours, already past the Express promise. The average Express parcel is a near-miss, which suggests the promise itself needs review, not just execution.

### (ii) Delay rate climbs steeply from Tier 1 to Tier 3

Tier 1 hubs run at 0.04, Tier 2 at 0.215 and Tier 3 at 0.44. Mean delivery hours move the same way: 48.5664, 58.8194 and 63.4943 (`outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`).

Each tier ships exactly 400 parcels in this history, so the gap is not a volume effect. It reflects how the hubs operate. Tier 3 should get buffer time, staffing and alert capacity first.

### (iii) Delay rate rises sharply with distance

| band | shipments | delayed | delay rate | mean hours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0-200 km | 274 | 6 | 0.0219 | 46.7559 |
| 201-400 km | 563 | 77 | 0.1368 | 55.966 |
| 401-600 km | 275 | 120 | 0.4364 | 64.266 |
| 601+ km | 88 | 75 | 0.8523 | 72.2608 |

Source: `outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`.

Short lanes are close to reliable and need no routine intervention. Past 400 km the promise stops holding, and in the 601+ band a parcel is more likely to be late than on time. Those lanes need a longer promise or a redesigned route, not more expediting.

### (iv) The three columns most correlated with delivery_hours

Ranked by absolute correlation with `delivery_hours` and excluding itself: `promised_hours` at +0.8372, `distance_km` at +0.4013, `n_stops` at +0.3247 (`outputs/part_a_correlations.csv`).

Most of the variation in delivery time is the service class the customer bought, so the largest lever is which services are sold on which lanes. Distance is the biggest physical driver after that, which makes re-cutting long lanes or fulfilling from a closer hub a direct reduction in hours. Route density matters too, so capping stops per route is something a hub manager can act on this week.

### (v) Why declared_value_inr was log-transformed

On the median-filled column the mean is INR 1,533.05 against a median of INR 1,121.50. After the natural log the mean is 7.0277 and the median 7.0224 (`outputs/part_a_checks.csv`).

A mean well above the median means a few very high-value parcels were dragging the column. The log brings an ordinary parcel and a rare expensive one onto the same scale, so declared value is not read mainly from the outliers.

## Model results (regression)

The table split 840 / 180 / 180 for training, validation and test. The leakage-check scaler was fitted on the 840 training rows only. Its largest absolute column mean is 0.0 on the scaled training rows and 0.2187 on the scaled test rows (`outputs/part_b_checks.csv`). A test-row mean clearly off zero is what shows no test statistic went into the scaler.

Five candidates, all fitted on the training rows and scored on the same 180 validation rows (`outputs/part_b_metrics.csv`):

| model | MAE | MSE | RMSE | R² |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mean_baseline | 15.7952 | 355.5204 | 18.8552 | -0.0212 |
| linear_regression | 3.9222 | 23.9235 | 4.8912 | 0.9313 |
| decision_tree | 5.0971 | 39.4338 | 6.2796 | 0.8867 |
| random_forest | 4.2882 | 30.355 | 5.5095 | 0.9128 |
| gradient_boosting | 3.9166 | 24.1722 | 4.9165 | 0.9306 |

### Baseline

Predicting the training mean for every parcel gives R² of -0.0212 and RMSE of 18.8552 hours. R² is the share of variation in delivery hours a model explains, so a slightly negative value means this rule explains none of it and does marginally worse than the validation rows' own average.

Its MAE is 15.7952 hours. A planner guessing the average is off by about sixteen hours on a typical parcel, which is no basis for committing to a 36-hour or 60-hour promise. Every model below has to beat this to be worth running.

### Validation winner

Linear regression has the lowest validation RMSE at 4.8912 hours, ahead of gradient boosting at 4.9165, random forest at 5.5095 and the decision tree at 6.2796. Its R² is 0.9313.

A typical estimate lands within about five hours of the real outcome. That is tight enough to plan a dispatch slot against a 36-hour promise, but not tight enough to treat a parcel sitting five hours inside its promise as safe.

### Tree depth: bias and variance

| max_depth | train_rmse | val_rmse | gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 11.6334 | 12.1324 | 0.4990 |
| 2 | 9.3687 | 10.4114 | 1.0427 |
| 3 | 7.5295 | 8.7033 | 1.1738 |
| 4 | 6.0999 | 7.3896 | 1.2897 |
| 6 | 4.7114 | 6.2796 | 1.5682 |
| 8 | 3.336 | 6.7901 | 3.4541 |
| 12 | 1.3613 | 7.354 | 5.9927 |
| unlimited | 0.0 | 7.8368 | 7.8368 |

Source: `outputs/part_b_tree_depth.csv`. The gap is `val_rmse` minus `train_rmse`.

Depths 1 to 3 are high-bias. The gap is small, only 0.4990 at depth 1, but both errors are high. The tree is not failing to generalise; it is too simple to describe the data. A depth-1 tree is wrong by over 11 hours even on rows it trained on.

Depths 8, 12 and unlimited are high-variance. Training error falls to 3.336, 1.3613 and 0.0 while validation error climbs to 6.7901, 7.354 and 7.8368, widening the gap to 7.8368. The unlimited tree reproduces its training rows exactly and is still the worst of the eight on unseen parcels.

Depth 6 is the turning point, with the lowest validation RMSE at 6.2796. That is the depth used for the `decision_tree` candidate above.

### Learning rate

With 100 trees held fixed, 0.1 wins at validation RMSE 4.9165 and R² 0.9306. At 0.01 the figures are 10.0018 and 0.7127; at 1.0 they are 7.3946 and 0.8429 (`outputs/part_b_gb_learning_rate.csv`).

At 0.01 each tree's correction is shrunk to a hundredth of its size, so 100 trees cannot walk the ensemble down to the signal. It is still underfit when the tree budget runs out. At 1.0 each tree applies its full correction, so the ensemble overshoots at every step and chases the previous tree's residuals instead of settling. Both failures come from the same capacity, which is the practical point: tree count and learning rate have to be chosen together.

### Cross-validation against validation

The two rankings disagree on first place.

| ranking | first | second |
| --- | --- | --- |
| validation RMSE | linear_regression, 4.8912 | gradient_boosting, 4.9165 |
| 5-fold CV mean R² | gradient_boosting, 0.9353 | linear_regression, 0.9261 |

The cross-validation ran on the 1,020 training-plus-validation rows with one shared `KFold`. Standard deviations were 0.0045 for gradient boosting and 0.0048 for linear regression (`outputs/part_b_cv.csv`).

The brief's rule picks the lowest validation RMSE, so linear regression stays the final model and is the one scored on test.

I would ship gradient boosting. Cross-validation averages five folds over 1,020 rows, so every row is held out once, while the validation ranking rests on a single 180-row split. The validation gap that put linear regression first is 0.0253 hours, about ninety seconds, well inside what one unlucky split produces. Both standard deviations are small, so the fold ordering is stable. For a margin this thin I would trust the estimate built on more held-out data, and I would confirm on a second split before changing production.

### Test result

Scored once on the 180 untouched test rows, linear regression gives MAE 3.5671, MSE 20.4624, RMSE 4.5235 and R² 0.9493.

The test score is slightly better than the validation score, which indicates the model is not over-tuned to the validation split. Planners get a dispatch-time estimate typically within three and a half hours of the outcome. That is dependable for scheduling and capacity work, but it is not a per-parcel promise-breach signal. That is the classifier's job.

## Model results (classification)

The shipped configuration keeps 7 of the 17 columns with `SelectKBest` and feeds a 100-tree random forest with no depth limit and `min_samples_leaf` 1, alerting at probability 0.5 (`outputs/part_c_final_config.csv`). It came from the `default_forest_same_7_features` row of the tuning table.

Four models on the 180 validation rows at the default 0.5 rule (`outputs/part_c_models.csv`):

| model | TN | FP | FN | TP | accuracy | precision | recall | f1 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| blind_all_on_time | 138 | 0 | 42 | 0 | 0.7667 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| logistic_regression | 128 | 10 | 7 | 35 | 0.9056 | 0.7778 | 0.8333 | 0.8046 |
| decision_tree_depth_6 | 128 | 10 | 11 | 31 | 0.8833 | 0.7561 | 0.7381 | 0.747 |
| random_forest_default | 132 | 6 | 11 | 31 | 0.9056 | 0.8378 | 0.7381 | 0.7848 |

### Blind baseline

Predicting "on time" for everything scores 0.7667 accuracy and 0.0 recall. TP is 0 and FN is 42, so all 42 delayed parcels in the validation split go unflagged.

The accuracy looks respectable only because 138 of the 180 parcels really were on time. A rule that never raises an alarm is right about those and wrong about every parcel operations cares about. No call gets placed and no hub manager gets warned, which is why accuracy cannot be the headline metric when under a quarter of cases are positive.

### Noise columns

The five bookkeeping columns hold a combined 0.2245 of the forest's impurity importance, roughly a fifth. All five fall outside the top 7 by F-score (`outputs/part_c_feature_scores.csv`):

| column | f_score | f_rank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| label_print_batch | 4.5847 | 8 |
| tape_roll_no | 1.9052 | 12 |
| sticker_serial_mod | 1.2776 | 14 |
| shelf_slot_no | 0.1393 | 16 |
| scanner_build_no | 0.0230 | 17 |

Every one is under 10, against 36.7281 for the weakest of the top seven.

They still collect importance because impurity importance rewards columns with many distinct values even when those values mean nothing. A batch number spanning 1000 to 9999, a roll number up to 500 and a continuous sticker serial give the tree hundreds of candidate split points, and some split will separate a few delayed training parcels by chance. The F-test asks a narrower question, whether delayed and on-time parcels differ on the column at all, and answers no. A feature-importance chart is therefore not evidence for keeping a field.

### How many columns to keep

Validation F1 by k: 0.6988 at 5, 0.8333 at 7, 0.7857 at 10, 0.8049 at 12, 0.7848 at 17 (`outputs/part_c_selectk.csv`).

The table brackets the answer from both sides. Five columns is too few and drops something that matters. Every k above 7 readmits bookkeeping columns and scores lower. All 17 columns score 0.7848 against 0.8333 for the best 7, which is the case for keeping the selector in the pipeline.

### Tuning

On the same 7 columns, validation F1 was 0.775 for the grid search and 0.7848 for the randomized search, against 0.8333 for the untuned forest (`outputs/part_c_tuning.csv`). The untuned forest won, so it is the shipped configuration.

This is not evidence that tuning fails. The searches optimise cross-validated accuracy on the training rows, reaching 0.8952 and 0.8905 across 135 and 45 fits. The comparison above is F1 on 180 validation rows holding 42 delayed parcels. Different metric, different data. Accuracy rewards getting the 138 on-time parcels right while F1 tracks only the delayed ones, and on a base of 42 a few parcels shifting between TP and FN can reverse the order. The conclusion is that this validation split is too small to settle a difference this fine. Both searches converged on `max_depth` 6, and with more labelled history, or with the searches scored on F1, the result could flip back.

### Alert threshold

The operations rule takes the highest threshold whose validation recall still reaches 0.80, because a missed delayed parcel costs more than an unnecessary call. That gives 0.5, with validation precision 0.7955 and recall 0.8333 (`outputs/part_c_thresholds.csv`). It catches 35 of 42 delayed parcels, misses 7, and raises 9 false alarms.

At 0.6 recall drops to 0.7619 and fails the rule, even though precision rises to 0.8889 and F1 to 0.8205, the best F1 in the table. Moving from 0.5 to 0.6 saves five wasted calls, taking FP from 9 to 4, but lets three more delayed parcels through, taking FN from 7 to 10. The rule rejects that trade. This is also why the threshold is not set by F1: the best-looking threshold is not the one operations can work with.

### Test result

Scored once on the 180 test rows at threshold 0.5: precision 0.6889, recall 0.7561, F1 0.7209 (`outputs/part_c_final_test.csv`). Roughly 7 of every 10 flagged parcels are genuinely delayed, and roughly 7.6 of every 10 delayed parcels get caught.

Test recall of 0.7561 is below the validation recall of 0.8333 that the threshold was chosen on, a drop of 0.0772, and it is below the 0.80 the operations rule asks for. Stating that directly: the alert as configured catches about three-quarters of delayed parcels, not the four-fifths intended.

The test set holds 41 delayed parcels, so one parcel moves recall by about 0.0244. The shortfall is 31 caught where 33 would have reached 0.8049, a difference of two parcels. That is well inside what a single split produces, so it is not evidence the model is broken, but it should not be smoothed over either. The threshold was not re-tuned against the test rows, since that would leave no clean estimate anywhere in the repository. The right response is to hold 0.5, quote recall as a range of roughly 0.76 to 0.83, and re-measure on real outcomes once a few hundred more parcels have been delivered.

## Operations recommendation

### The nine Escalate hubs

Clustering the 24 hubs on measured behaviour puts nine in the Escalate segment, averaging a delay rate of 0.46 against 0.0257 for the seven Standard hubs (`outputs/segment_profile.csv`). Worst first, with each hub's own rate:

| hub | city | tier | delay rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HUB19 | Guwahati | 3 | 0.62 |
| HUB24 | Agartala | 3 | 0.54 |
| HUB20 | Raipur | 3 | 0.48 |
| HUB13 | Nagpur | 2 | 0.46 |
| HUB17 | Patna | 3 | 0.46 |
| HUB23 | Siliguri | 3 | 0.44 |
| HUB21 | Bhubaneswar | 3 | 0.40 |
| HUB18 | Ranchi | 3 | 0.38 |
| HUB15 | Kochi | 2 | 0.36 |

Sources: `outputs/hub_profile.csv` and `outputs/hub_segments.csv`.

At Guwahati roughly three parcels in five miss the promise. These nine hubs are where buffer time, staffing and alert-handling capacity belong. Every hub ships the same 50 parcels in this history, so the spread is about operations, not load.

### Hubs whose tier label misleads

Four hubs sit in a segment their tier does not predict (`outputs/tier_vs_segment_mismatch.csv`):

| hub | city | tier label | measured segment | delay rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HUB07 | Kolkata | 1 | Watch | 0.14 |
| HUB13 | Nagpur | 2 | Escalate | 0.46 |
| HUB15 | Kochi | 2 | Escalate | 0.36 |
| HUB22 | Dehradun | 3 | Watch | 0.20 |

Two are worse than their label implies and two are better. Kolkata carries a Tier 1 label but runs at 0.14, more than five times the Standard segment's 0.0257, so treating it as metro-grade understates its risk. Nagpur and Kochi carry Tier 2 labels but run at 0.46 and 0.36. Dehradun is the reverse case, labelled Tier 3 but running at 0.20.

The recommendation is not to relabel these hubs. Tier should stay the planning label it already is, since it drives capacity, contracts and reporting, and changing it would ripple through all three. What should change is the playbook each hub runs, and the playbook should follow the measured segment. In practice that means Nagpur and Kochi move onto the Escalate playbook despite their Tier 2 label, Kolkata moves onto Watch despite Tier 1, and Dehradun steps down to Watch despite Tier 3.

The segments should be recomputed on a regular cadence. A hub that moves between segments is the earliest signal that something there has changed.

### What the threshold buys

The alert fires at a delay probability of 0.5. On the held-out test rows (`outputs/part_c_final_test.csv`):

- About 6.9 of every 10 flagged parcels are genuinely delayed, from a precision of 0.6889. Around three calls in ten go to a parcel that would have arrived on time.
- About 7.6 of every 10 delayed parcels are caught, from a recall of 0.7561. Close to a quarter still arrive late unflagged, so the alert list is a priority queue, not a guarantee.

The balance is set by the operations rule: take the strictest threshold that still catches at least 80% of delayed parcels on validation, because a missed parcel costs more than a wasted call. Moving up to 0.6 would improve precision but drop validation recall to 0.7619, under that floor (`outputs/part_c_thresholds.csv`).

### The next 48 parcels

Of the 48 parcels about to dispatch, 8 were flagged. Six need a hub-manager escalation because they sit at Escalate-segment hubs, and 2 need a proactive customer call. The remaining 40 need no action (`outputs/new_shipment_actions.csv`).

Eight interventions on a batch of 48 is small enough to clear the same day, which is the point of flagging at dispatch.

### Why K = 3

Separation keeps improving as clusters are added: 2.894 at K=2, 5.3928 at K=3, 6.3047 at K=4, 6.8866 at K=5 (`outputs/part_d_separation.csv`). On the ratio alone, more clusters look better.

K = 3 stays because operations runs three playbooks, and the better-separated solutions cut the network too finely to staff. K=4 gives sizes 8-7-5-4 and K=5 gives 8-7-4-3-2, each with a cluster under 6 hubs. A group of two or three hubs cannot justify writing, training and maintaining its own playbook, so the extra separation improves a number without changing anything actionable.

### (b) What to monitor

The triggers below are proposed values, to be confirmed with operations. Each baseline is a number this repository computed.

| quantity | baseline | proposed trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly delayed share of delivered parcels | 0.2317 (`outputs/part_a_checks.csv`) | Investigate above 0.28 or below 0.18. Escalate after two consecutive weeks above 0.28. |
| Weekly share of parcels flagged | 0.2444, from 9 false positives plus 35 true positives in 180 validation rows at threshold 0.5 (`outputs/part_c_thresholds.csv`) | Investigate outside 0.18 to 0.32. A flagged share that drifts while the delayed share holds steady points to the model's calibration, not the network. |
| Recall on parcels with known outcomes | 0.7561 (`outputs/part_c_final_test.csv`) | Alert below 0.70 over a rolling 200 delivered parcels. Treat below 0.65 as a retraining trigger. Report precision, baseline 0.6889, alongside it, since recall can always be bought by lowering the threshold. |

Recall and precision can only be measured once outcomes are known, so this needs a prediction log joined to delivery outcomes. That is the piece the repository does not yet have.

### (c) Retraining, the sale window, and drift

**Schedule.** Retrain quarterly, and additionally whenever a monitor in (b) trips. Each retrain should repeat the whole pipeline, including Part A's cleaning, Part C's selection and tuning, and the threshold rule, instead of refitting the existing configuration. The medians in `models/impute_values.csv`, the seven selected columns and the alert threshold are all fitted quantities that go stale. The threshold especially needs re-deriving from a fresh validation split under the same rule, since 0.5 was chosen on one split and its test recall of 0.7561 already missed the 0.80 target.

**The sale window.** `sale_event_flag` marks the 15 to 28 September sale, and it is one of the seven columns the model keeps, with an F-score of 36.7281 at rank 7 (`outputs/part_c_feature_scores.csv`). Two things follow.

First, sale-window performance has to be compared against the same kind of event, such as the previous year's sale or another promotional window. Comparing it against the ordinary weeks on either side would read a normal sale as a model failure, because delivery hours genuinely rise during one.

Second, this history contains a single sale window, so everything the model knows about sale behaviour comes from one occurrence. Sale-period predictions are the least trustworthy part of the model. The first sale after deployment should be treated as a measurement exercise with the threshold held fixed.

**Drift.** The model reads a parcel mostly through lane length, route density, service class and hub attributes, so drift will show up in those inputs before it shows up in the target.

- Distance. A rising `mean_distance_km` per hub against `outputs/hub_profile.csv`, or a shift in the distance-band mix away from the baseline counts of 274, 563, 275 and 88 parcels. Distance is the strongest single input at an F-score of 389.2131, so this matters most.
- Stops. A rising `mean_n_stops` per hub, meaning routes are loaded more densely than when the model was fitted.
- Service mix. A change in the split of 315 Economy, 243 Express and 642 Standard parcels (`outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`). Express is the hardest promise to keep, so a shift toward Express raises the delayed share with nothing operational having worsened.
- Rain share. A heavier monsoon than the one in this history. No output file currently carries a baseline rain share, so Part A's checks file needs extending before this monitor can be built.
- Hub capacity. Any change to `hub_daily_capacity`, which the model treats as a fixed hub attribute at an F-score of 144.1347. A depot expansion or closure invalidates the learned relationship at once and should force a retrain ahead of the quarterly cadence.

## Limitations

**1. The data is synthetic.** Every row comes from `generate_data.py`, which builds `delivery_hours` from a fixed formula plus normal noise. That is why the relationships are so clean: linear regression reaches a test R² of 0.9493, which no real last-mile network would produce. The pipeline, the leakage discipline and the threshold rule transfer to real data. The accuracy figures do not, and should be read as an upper bound.

**2. The test set holds only 41 delayed parcels.** With FN 10 and TP 31, one parcel moves recall by about 0.0244. The reported recall of 0.7561 therefore carries a few points of uncertainty either way, as does the precision of 0.6889. No decision should rest on a one or two point difference between this model and an alternative.

**3. The threshold was tuned on one 180-row split and did not hold.** Threshold 0.5 was picked as the highest value whose validation recall still reached 0.80, scoring 0.8333 there. On test the same threshold gave 0.7561, below the 0.80 the operations rule asks for. The alert as configured catches about three-quarters of delayed parcels, not four-fifths. It was not re-tuned against the test rows, since that would leave no clean estimate in the repository.

**4. Impurity importance cannot show a column is useful.** The five bookkeeping columns took a combined 0.2245 of the forest's importance while carrying no signal by construction. `label_print_batch` even scored a higher F-score, 4.5847 at rank 8, than three genuine columns. Feature decisions here should rest on the F-test and on held-out performance, not on an importance ranking.

**5. Fixing k = 7 discards five columns.** The selector keeps `distance_km`, `hub_tier`, `hub_daily_capacity`, `n_stops`, `promised_hours`, `rain_flag` and `sale_event_flag`. It drops `is_cod`, `is_weekend` and `dispatch_hour`, which correlate with `delivery_hours` at +0.0610, +0.1038 and +0.0300 (`outputs/part_a_correlations.csv`), along with `parcel_weight_kg` and `log_declared_value`. Whatever effect a COD payment, a weekend dispatch or a late-evening dispatch has on lateness is unavailable to the model as configured. k=7 won on validation F1, 0.8333 against 0.8049 at k=12 (`outputs/part_c_selectk.csv`), but that was one split, and a univariate F-test scores each column alone, so it cannot see a column that only matters in combination with another.

**6. Each hub profile rests on 50 parcels.** Every row of `outputs/hub_profile.csv` has `shipments` of 50, so a hub's delay rate moves 0.02 per parcel, and the segmentation is built on 24 such estimates. The boundary hubs are the fragile ones: Kochi at 0.36 is the mildest Escalate hub and Coimbatore at 0.24 the most severe Watch hub. Segment assignments should be treated as provisional and recomputed as volume builds, which is a further reason to keep tier as the stable planning label and let the segment drive only the playbook.

**7. One sale window and a single season.** `sale_event_flag` is fitted from one 15 to 28 September sale, and the history spans a single stretch of consecutive months, so the model has seen no full-year seasonality. There is no second monsoon and no festive peak outside this one window. Any prediction for a period unlike the training period is extrapolation, and sale periods are the clearest case.
